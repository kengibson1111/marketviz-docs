# Sector Cycle Map

This reference maps the four phases of the business cycle to the sectors and industries that historically perform best and worst in each. Use it as a lookup table when the [Cyclical Rotation Playbook](/insights/playbook/cyclical-rotation.md) identifies the current cycle phase and direction.

The mappings reflect decades of market data and economic logic, but they are historical tendencies — not guarantees. Every cycle has unique characteristics, and sector leadership can deviate from historical norms.

---

## Business Cycle Phases (Overview)

| Phase | Economic Characteristics | Typical Duration |
|-------|-------------------------|-----------------|
| **Early Expansion** | Recovery from recession. GDP accelerating, unemployment falling, credit loosening, consumer confidence rebuilding. | 12–18 months |
| **Late Expansion** | Economy near peak. Growth slowing at the margin, inflation rising, labor market tight, Fed tightening or rates elevated. | 12–24 months |
| **Early Contraction** | Recession beginning. GDP decelerating or negative, layoffs starting, credit tightening, earnings revisions negative. | 6–12 months |
| **Late Contraction** | Recession deepening or bottoming. Maximum pessimism, high unemployment, Fed cutting rates, inventories depleted. | 6–12 months |

---

## Phase 1: Early Expansion (Recovery)

The economy is rebounding. Monetary policy is accommodative, credit is loosening, and pent-up demand is being released.

### Sectors That Historically Outperform

| Sector | Why It Works | Key Industries |
|--------|-------------|----------------|
| **Consumer Discretionary** | Consumers regain confidence and resume spending on non-essentials. Pent-up demand from the recession gets released. | Retail, autos, homebuilders, restaurants, travel & leisure |
| **Financials** | Loan growth accelerates as businesses and consumers borrow again. Credit losses peak and decline. Yield curve is often steep (short rates low, long rates higher), boosting net interest margins. | Regional banks, diversified financials, insurance, capital markets |
| **Industrials** | Inventory restocking, infrastructure spending, and deferred capital projects restart. Manufacturing PMI rebounds. | Machinery, aerospace & defense, construction, railroads |
| **Real Estate** | Low interest rates drive housing demand and commercial leasing activity. REITs benefit from asset value recovery. | Residential REITs, homebuilders, commercial property |
| **Information Technology** | Business capex on technology resumes. Companies invest in efficiency after cutting during the downturn. | Semiconductors, enterprise software, IT services, cloud infrastructure |

### Sectors That Historically Underperform (Relative)

| Sector | Why It Lags |
|--------|-------------|
| **Utilities** | Defensive yield plays get rotated out of as risk appetite returns. Rising rate expectations hurt their bond-like valuation. |
| **Consumer Staples** | Defensive holdings that outperformed during the recession see relative selling as investors move to higher-beta plays. |
| **Healthcare** | Defensive characteristics become less attractive; relative performance lags more aggressive sectors. |

## Phase 2: Late Expansion (Peak Approaching)

Growth is maturing. Inflation is building, the labor market is tight, and monetary policy is tightening or already restrictive.

### Sectors That Historically Outperform

| Sector | Why It Works | Key Industries |
|--------|-------------|----------------|
| **Energy** | Demand for oil and commodities peaks as global economic activity is at maximum. Supply constraints from years of underinvestment amplify price spikes. | Oil & gas exploration, oilfield services, refiners, pipelines |
| **Materials** | Industrial demand for raw materials (metals, chemicals, construction inputs) reaches cyclical highs. Pricing power is strong. | Mining, steel, specialty chemicals, packaging |
| **Industrials** | Late-cycle infrastructure and defense spending continues. Order backlogs are full. | Aerospace & defense, electrical equipment, construction |
| **Healthcare** | Begins transitioning to outperformance as investors rotate toward quality and defensiveness while growth is still present. | Pharmaceuticals, managed care, medical devices, biotech |

### Sectors That Historically Underperform (Relative)

| Sector | Why It Lags |
|--------|-------------|
| **Consumer Discretionary** | Rising interest rates and inflation squeeze consumer wallets. Housing and auto affordability decline. |
| **Information Technology** | High-multiple growth stocks get hit by rising discount rates. Valuation compression begins. |
| **Real Estate** | Rising interest rates directly increase borrowing costs, compressing property values and REIT yields. |

---

## Phase 3: Early Contraction (Recession Beginning)

The economy is rolling over. Earnings revisions turn sharply negative, credit markets tighten, and layoffs begin.

### Sectors That Historically Outperform

| Sector | Why It Works | Key Industries |
|--------|-------------|----------------|
| **Consumer Staples** | Demand for essential goods (food, household products, personal care) is recession-resistant. Stable cash flows and dividends attract defensive capital. | Food & beverage, household products, tobacco, discount retail |
| **Healthcare** | Medical spending is non-discretionary. Aging demographics provide structural demand regardless of economic conditions. | Pharmaceuticals, managed care, medical devices, healthcare facilities |
| **Utilities** | Regulated revenue streams are recession-proof. High dividend yields attract capital fleeing volatile sectors. Bond-proxy characteristics shine when rates are being cut. | Electric utilities, gas utilities, water utilities, regulated power |
| **Communication Services (Defensive)** | Subscription-based telecom services have sticky demand. Consumers cut luxury spending before canceling internet/phone. | Telecom carriers, cable/broadband providers |

### Sectors That Historically Underperform (Relative)

| Sector | Why It Lags |
|--------|-------------|
| **Consumer Discretionary** | Consumers cut non-essential spending first. Auto sales, luxury goods, and travel collapse. |
| **Financials** | Loan defaults rise, credit losses spike, and investment banking revenues dry up. |
| **Industrials** | Capital spending gets deferred. Order cancellations increase. Manufacturing PMI contracts. |
| **Energy** | Demand destruction as economic activity declines. Oil prices typically fall. |
| **Materials** | Industrial demand collapses. Commodity prices fall. Excess inventory builds. |

---

## Phase 4: Late Contraction (Bottoming / Maximum Pessimism)

The recession is deepening or nearing its bottom. Unemployment is high, the Fed is cutting aggressively, and sentiment is at maximum pessimism. This is historically the best time to position for the next recovery.

### Sectors That Begin Outperforming (Leading the Recovery)

| Sector | Why It Works | Key Industries |
|--------|-------------|----------------|
| **Consumer Discretionary** | The earliest recovery plays. Stocks price in recovery 6–9 months before the economy turns. Valuations are depressed and expectations are low. | Homebuilders, auto manufacturers, specialty retail, restaurants |
| **Financials** | Credit losses begin peaking. Reserve releases will flow to earnings in the next phase. Yield curve steepens as the Fed cuts short rates. | Banks (especially regionals), diversified financials, insurance |
| **Information Technology** | Technology capex is the first discretionary budget to return. Secular growth themes (cloud, AI, automation) resume. Valuations reset to attractive levels during the recession. | Semiconductors, software, IT services |

### Sectors That Begin Underperforming (Rotation Away)

| Sector | Why It Lags |
|--------|-------------|
| **Utilities** | Defensive premium erodes as risk appetite returns. Relative outperformance peaks before the recession ends. |
| **Consumer Staples** | Same dynamic as utilities — money rotates into higher-beta recovery plays. |

| Sector | Cyclicality | Notes |
|--------|-------------|-------|
| Consumer Discretionary | Very High | Directly tied to consumer confidence and spending |
| Financials | High | Tied to credit cycles, loan growth, and interest rates |
| Industrials | High | Tied to capital spending and manufacturing activity |
| Materials | High | Tied to commodity demand and industrial production |
| Energy | High | Tied to global demand and commodity pricing |
| Information Technology | Moderate-High | Business capex driven, but secular growth offsets some cyclicality |

## Important Caveats

- **Sector composition changes:** The mix of companies within each sector evolves. Today's Information Technology sector is very different from 2005's. Always verify that the sector's current constituents match the historical logic.
- **Interest rate regime matters:** The typical playbook assumes a normal rate cycle. In prolonged zero-rate or inverted-curve environments, historical sector rotation patterns can break down.
- **Global vs. domestic:** Multinational companies within a sector may respond to global cycle conditions rather than domestic. An industrial company with 70% international revenue may outperform during a domestic recession if global growth remains strong.
- **Secular trends override:** Structural shifts (energy transition, AI adoption, demographic aging) can override cyclical patterns for specific industries within a sector.
- **Not all companies within a sector behave the same:** A high-quality consumer staples company with pricing power behaves very differently from a low-margin private-label competitor during a recession. Use the individual metric docs to differentiate within sectors.
